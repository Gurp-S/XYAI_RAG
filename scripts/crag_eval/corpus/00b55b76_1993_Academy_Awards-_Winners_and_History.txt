      <table width="80%" border="0" align="center" cellpadding="1" cellspacing="0">
        <tr>
          <td><center>
            <p><strong><font size="5">1993</font></strong> <br />
                <font size="2">The winner is listed first, in CAPITAL letters.</font></p>
            <p><font size="2"><i><a href="1993.html">Filmsite's Greatest Films
            of 1993</a></i></font> </p>
          </center>
          
              <table width="90%" border="1" align="center" cellpadding="1" cellspacing="0" bordercolor="#CCCCCC">
                <tr>
                  <td colspan="5"><center>
                      <b><font size="2">Best Picture </font></b>
                  </center></td>
                </tr>
                <tr>
                  <td width="20%"><center>
                      <img src="titles/schindlerslist-title.jpg" width="160" height="119" /><br />
                      <font size="2"><a href="schi.html"> SCHINDLER'S LIST (1993) </a></font>
                  </center></td>
                  <td width="20%"><center>
                      <img src="titles/fugitive.jpg" alt="" width="160" height="122" /><br />
                      <font size="2">The Fugitive (1993) </font>
                  </center></td>
                  <td width="20%"><center>
                      <img src="titles/innameoffather.jpg" alt="" width="160" height="126" /><br />
                      <font size="2">In the Name of the Father (1993, UK/Ireland) </font>
                  </center></td>
                  <td width="20%"><center>
                      <img src="titles/piano1993.jpg" alt="" width="160" height="120" /><br />
                      <font size="2">The Piano (1993, NZ/Australia/Fr.) </font>
                  </center></td>
                  <td width="20%"><center>
                      <img src="titles/remainsofday.jpg" alt="" width="160" height="127" /><br />
                      <font size="2">The Remains of the Day (1993, UK) </font>
                  </center></td>
                </tr>
              </table>
      <p><font size="2"><b>Actor:</b><br />
                TOM HANKS in &quot;Philadelphia&quot;, Daniel Day-Lewis in &quot;In
                the Name of the Father&quot;, Laurence Fishburne in &quot;What's
                Love Got to Do With It&quot;, Anthony Hopkins in &quot;The Remains
                of the Day&quot;, Liam Neeson in <a href="schi.html">&quot;Schindler's
                  List&quot;</a><br />
              <b>Actress:</b><br />
                HOLLY HUNTER in &quot;The Piano&quot;, Angela Bassett in &quot;What's
                Love Got to Do With It&quot;, Stockard Channing in &quot;Six
                Degrees of Separation&quot;, Emma Thompson in &quot;The Remains
                of the Day&quot;, Debra Winger in &quot;Shadowlands&quot;<br />
      <b>Supporting Actor:</b><br />
                TOMMY LEE JONES in &quot;The Fugitive&quot;, Leonardo DiCaprio
                in &quot;What's Eating Gilbert Grape&quot;, Ralph Fiennes in <a href="schi.html">&quot;Schindler's
                  List&quot;</a>, John Malkovich in &quot;In the Line of Fire&quot;,
                Pete Postlethwaite in &quot;In the Name of the Father&quot;<br />
              <b>Supporting Actress:</b><br />
                ANNA PAQUIN in &quot;The Piano&quot;, Holly Hunter in &quot;The
                Firm&quot;, Rosie Perez in &quot;Fearless&quot;, Winona Ryder
                in &quot;The Age of Innocence&quot;, Emma Thompson in &quot;In
                the Name of the Father&quot;<br />
      <b>Director:</b> <br />
                STEVEN SPIELBERG for <a href="schi.html">&quot;Schindler's
                  List&quot;</a>, Robert Altman for &quot;Short Cuts&quot;, Jane
                Campion for &quot;The Piano&quot;, James Ivory for &quot;The
                Remains of the Day&quot;, Jim Sheridan for 
                &quot;In the Name of the Father&quot;</font></p>
              
                <hr width="90%" />
                
                <p><font size="2"><img src="posters/schindlerslist.jpg" width="375" height="555" align="right" />Finally,
                  after many years of dubious treatment and neglect, producer/director
                  Steven Spielberg (with his <i>sixth</i> directorial and <i>seventh</i> Best
                  Picture nomination) won the Best Picture Oscar (and Best Director
                  Oscar) for his monumental, mature masterpiece <a href="schi.html"><b>Schindler's
                    List</b></a>. [Note: The film - although mostly black and
                    white - contained a few segments in color, thereby disqualifying
                    it from being the most recent completely black and white
                    film to win the Best Picture Oscar. That honor still applied
                    to <a href="apar.html"><b>The
                  Apartment (1960)</b></a>.]</font></p>
                <p><font size="2">Spielberg won his <i>first</i> competitive Oscars
                  for the powerful, documentary-style, 'historical' dramatization
                  of Thomas Keneally's 1982 book (from a screenplay by Steven
                  Zaillian) about the Third Reich's Holocaust, and the role of
                  one complex man named Schindler (Liam Neeson) - a failed German
                  industrialist and Catholic war profiteer (a pots-and-pans factory
                  owner), who struggled to save more than a thousand Polish-Jewish
                  lives in Nazi-occupied Poland with the assistance of Jewish
                  accountant and confidante Itzhak Stern (Ben Kingsley).</font></p>
                <p><font size="2">The three hour-long, small-budget ($23 million)
                  film had <i>twelve</i> nominations and <i>seven</i> overall
                  wins (Best Picture, Best Director, Best Screenplay, Best Art
                  Direction, Best Cinematography for Janusz Kaminski, Best Film
                  Editing, and Best Original Score - by John Williams). Surprisingly,
                  the film won no acting awards. Spielberg also triumphed in
                  the same year with three technical-achievement awards for his
                  box-office hit <b><a href="series-jurassicpark.html">Jurassic
                    Park</a></b>: Best Sound, Best Sound Effects Editing, and Best
                  Visual Effects.</font></p>
                <p><font size="2"> Four of the five Best Picture nominees were
                  serious-minded films. The one exception was the box-office
                  hit and action thriller <b>The Fugitive</b> (with seven nominations
                  and one win - Best Supporting Actor) - the story of the relentless
                  tracking of a convicted innocent man in director Andrew Davis'
                  re-make of the 60's long-running TV series - it was notable
                  as the <i>first</i> - and only - Best Picture nominee remake
                  to be based on a popular TV series.</font></p>
                <p><font size="2">The other three Best Picture nominees included:</font></p>
                <ul>
                  <li><font size="2">the strange love story between a mute-by-choice
                    19th century Scotswoman (and pianist) and a New Zealand neighbor
                    in producer/writer/director Jane Campion's offbeat film <b>The
                      Piano</b> (with eight nominations and three wins - Best Actress,
                    Best Supporting Actress, and Best Screenplay)</font></li>
                  <li><font size="2">director Jim Sheridan's political-injustice
                    drama about an accused IRA bomber who is wrongfully imprisoned
                    in <b>In the Name of the Father</b> (with seven nominations
                    and no wins)</font></li>
                  <li><font size="2">the Merchant Ivory period drama of unrequited
                    love in director James Ivory's film <b>The Remains of the
                      Day</b> (with eight nominations and no wins)</font></li>
                </ul>
                <p><font size="2">Andrew Davis, the director of Best Picture-nominated <b>The
                  Fugitive</b>, failed to receive a nomination in the Best
                  Director category. Davis' place was filled by director Robert
                  Altman for his three-hour long film, a collage of Raymond
                  Carver's short stories, <b>Short Cuts</b> (the film's <i>sole</i> nomination).
                  [This was Altman's second consecutive Best Director nomination
                  for a film that did not receive a Best Picture nomination
                  - <b>The Player (1992)</b> also received a Best Director
                  nomination without a Best Picture nod. And it was also Altman's
                  fourth unsuccessful bid to be Best Director.]</font></p>
                <p><font size="2">Jane Campion's nomination as Best Director for <b>The
                  Piano</b> made her only the <i>second</i> woman in Oscar
                  history to be nominated in the category. Campion is the first
                  (and only) woman to have directed a Best Picture nominee
                  AND to have received a Best Director nomination for herself.
                  [The <i>first</i> woman ever nominated for Best Director
                  was Lina Wertmuller for <b>Seven Beauties (1976)</b>, seventeen
                  years earlier.] Although Campion lost the Best Director Oscar,
                  she won an Oscar for Best Original Screenplay.</font></p>
                <p><font size="2">Half of all the male acting nominations went
                  to British performers (Day-Lewis, Hopkins, Neeson, Postlethwaite,
                  and Fiennes). All four of the year's acting winners were first-time
                  Academy Award winners.</font></p>
                <p><font size="2">The Best Actor Oscar was presented to Tom Hanks
                  (with his second nomination and <i>first</i> Oscar win), best-known
                  for comedic roles, for his serious performance as AIDS-infected
                  corporate attorney and victim Andrew Beckett, fired from his
                  job and fighting homophobia in director Jonathan Demme's bold
                  adaptation of Ron Nyswaner's screenplay for <b>Philadelphia</b> (with
                  five nominations and two wins - the <i>second</i> Oscar was
                  for Bruce Springsteen's Best Song &quot;Streets of Philadelphia&quot;).
                  In the same year, Hanks starred in Nora Ephron's very popular
                  romantic comedy <b>Sleepless in Seattle</b>, a remake of <b>An
                    Affair to Remember (1957)</b> - with two unsuccessful nominations
                  for Best Original Screenplay and Best Original Song. [Note:
                  During Hanks' acceptance speech, he paid homage to his high
                  school gay teacher Rawley Farnsworth - the situation was later
                  used as the basis for the comedy <b>In &amp; Out (1997)</b> in
                  which a passionate Oscar winner during his acceptance speech
                  inadvertently outed a teacher.]</font></p>
                <p><font size="2">The other Best Actor nominees were:</font></p>
                <ul>
                  <li><font size="2">Daniel Day-Lewis (with his second nomination)
                    as wrongly-imprisoned IRA terrorist and political prisoner
                    Gerald Conlon in <b>In the Name of the Father</b></font></li>
                  <li><font size="2">Laurence Fishburne (with his first nomination)
                    as abusive wife-beater Ike Turner in <b>What's Love Got to
                      Do With It</b> (with two nominations and no wins); Fishburne,
                    whose nomination was bumped up to a lead one, performed his
                    own songs in the film</font></li>
                  <li><font size="2">Anthony Hopkins (with his second nomination)
                    as faithful, yet repressed head butler Stevens in <b>The
                      Remains of the Day</b></font></li>
                  <li><font size="2">N. Ireland native Liam Neeson (with his first
                    nomination) as conscience-stricken German industrialist Oskar
                    Schindler, the savior of a thousand Polish Jews in <a href="schi.html"><b>Schindler's
                      List</b></a></font></li>
                </ul>
                <p><font size="2">Among the female acting nominees, two were simultaneously
                  nominated in the lead and supporting acting categories in 1993
                  - Holly Hunter and Emma Thompson. They were the <i>first</i> nominees
                  to compete against each other in <i>both</i> the Actress and
                  Supporting Actress categories, in the same year:</font></p>
                <blockquote>
                  <p><font size="2">[Because Emma Thompson lost <i>both</i> Oscars
                    in the Best Actress and Best Supporting Actress categories,
                    she shares the same dubious 'achievement' with actress Sigourney
                    Weaver - who was also unsuccessful with her nominations for <b>Gorillas
                      in the Mist (1988)</b> and <b>Working Girl (1988)</b>. Although
                    Thompson and Weaver both lost, usually a double-nominee wins
                    one of the categories.]</font></p>
                </blockquote>
                <p><font size="2">Holly Hunter (with her <i>second/third</i> nominations
                  and <i>first</i> Oscar win) won the Best Actress Oscar for
                  her dialogue-less performance as the 19th century mute (since
                  childhood) and mail-order bride Ada McGrath - the erotic, Scottish
                  woman and gifted pianist in <b>The Piano</b>. [This was another
                  instance in which an award was given for an actress' non-speaking
                  role, i.e., Jane Wyman in <b>Johnny Belinda (1948)</b>, Patty
                  Duke in <b>The Miracle Worker (1962)</b>, Marlee Matlin in <b>Children
                  of a Lesser God (1986)</b>.]</font></p>
                <p><font size="2">The other four Best Actress nominees were:</font></p>
                <ul>
                  <li><font size="2">Angela Bassett (with her first nomination)
                    as abused rock singer Tina Turner in <b>What's Love Got to
                      Do With It</b></font></li>
                  <li><font size="2">Stockard Channing (with her first nomination)
                    as privileged Fifth Avenue wife Ouisa Kittredge in director
                    Fred Schepisi's satirical comedy/drama of race and class,<b> Six
                      Degrees of Separation</b> (the film's <i>sole</i> nomination)</font></li>
                  <li><font size="2">Emma Thompson (with her second - or third
                    nomination) as housekeeper Miss Kenton in <b>The Remains
                      of the Day</b></font></li>
                  <li><font size="2">Debra Winger (with her third unsuccessful
                    nomination) as Joy Gresham, the forthright American fan/poet
                    and romantic lover of Oxford author/lecturer C. S. Lewis
                    in director Richard Attenborough's exquisite melodrama, <b>Shadowlands</b> (with
                    only two nominations, also Best Adapted Screenplay - and
                    no wins)</font></li>
                </ul>
                <p><font size="2">The Best Supporting Actor Oscar was won by Tommy
                  Lee Jones (with his second nomination and <i>first</i> Oscar
                  win) for his performance as determined and relentless, laconic
                  Deputy U.S. Marshal Samuel Gerard who tracks fugitive Harrison
                  Ford in the exciting chase film <b>The Fugitive</b>. Many felt
                  the award should have gone to Ralph Fiennes instead.</font></p>
                <p><font size="2">The other four Best Supporting Actor nominees
                  in the category were:</font></p>
                <ul>
                  <li><font size="2">nineteen year-old (pre-<b>Titanic</b>) Leonardo
                    DiCaprio (with his first nomination) as co-star Johnny Depp's
                    impaired, mentally-challenged teenage brother Arnie Grape
                    in director Lasse Hallstrom's <b>What's Eating Gilbert Grape</b> (the
                    film's sole nomination)</font></li>
                  <li><font size="2">Ralph Fiennes (with his first nomination)
                    as the merciless and vicious Nazi labor camp commander Amon
                    Goeth in Plaszow in <a href="schi.html"><b>Schindler's
                      List</b></a></font></li>
                  <li><font size="2">John Malkovich (with his second nomination)
                    as former CIA agent and cold-blooded Presidential assassin
                    Mitch Leary in director Wolfgang Petersen's thriller <b>In
                      the Line of Fire</b> (with three nominations and no wins)</font></li>
                  <li><font size="2">Pete Postlethwaite (with his first nomination)
                    as Guiseppe Conlon, the father of wrongly-imprisoned co-star
                    Daniel Day-Lewis in <b>In the Name of the Father</b></font></li>
                </ul>
                <p><font size="2">The unexpected winner of the Best Supporting
                  Actress Oscar was 11 year-old Anna Paquin for her R-rated film
                  role as co-star (and competitive nominee) Holly Hunter's illegitimate
                  daughter Flora in <b>The Piano</b>. [Paquin's Oscar, in her
                  debut film, made her the <i>second</i> youngest competitive
                  Oscar winner, second to 10 year-old Tatum O'Neal who won the
                  Oscar twenty years earlier for <b>Paper Moon (1973)</b>. Paquin
                  also became the <i>first</i> New Zealander to receive an Oscar
                  for Best Supporting Actress.]</font></p>
                <p><font size="2">The other Best Supporting Actress nominees included:</font></p>
                <ul>
                  <li><font size="2">Holly Hunter (with her second - or third nomination)
                    as tough, bleach-blonde secretary Tammy Hemphill in producer/director
                    Sydney Pollack's thriller based on John Grisham's best-selling
                    novel about a corrupted law firm, <b>The Firm</b> (with two
                    nominations and no wins)</font></li>
                  <li><font size="2">Brooklyn-born Puerto Rican Rosie Perez (with
                    her first nomination) as co-star Jeff Bridges' fellow airplane
                    crash survivor and guilt-stricken Hispanic mother Carla Rodrigo
                    in director Peter Weir's <b>Fearless</b> (the film's <i>sole</i> nomination)</font></li>
                  <li><font size="2">Winona Ryder (with her first nomination and
                    the favored nominee) as May Welland - the emotionally-proper
                    fiancee of co-star Daniel Day-Lewis in director Martin Scorsese's
                    film adaptation of Edith Wharton's novel <b>The Age of Innocence</b> (with
                    five nominations and one win - Best Costume Design)</font></li>
                  <li><font size="2">Emma Thompson (with her second - or third
                    nomination) as Gareth Peirce - Gerald Conlon's (Daniel Day-Lewis')
                    English solicitor in <b>In the Name of the Father</b></font></li>
                </ul>
                <p><font size="2">Six-time Best Actress nominee (in 1949, 1953,
                  1956-1958, and 1960) and win-less Deborah Kerr received an
                  Honorary award during the ceremonies, 
                  &quot;in appreciation for a full career's worth of elegant and
                  beautifully-crafted performances.&quot; She had appeared in some
                  of cinema's greatest films, including <b><a href="blacknarcissus.html">Black Narcissus (1947)</a></b>, <b>I
                    See A Dark Stranger (1947)</b>, <b>Edward, My Son (1949)</b>, <b>The
                      Prisoner of Zenda (1952)</b>, <a href="from.html"><b>From Here
                        to Eternity (1953)</b></a>, <a href="kingi.html"><b>The King
                          and I (1956)</b></a>, <b>Tea and Sympathy (1956)</b>, <b>Heaven
                            Knows, Mr. Allison (1957)</b>, <b>Separate Tables (1958)</b>, <b>The
                Night of the Iguana (1964)</b>, and <b>The Arrangement (1969)</b>.</font></p>
                <p><font size="2"><b>Oscar Snubs and Omissions:</b></font></p>
                <p><font size="2">Brad Pitt was un-nominated for his performance
                  as terrifying, homicidal serial killer Early Grayce in <b>Kalifornia</b>,
                  nor was Val Kilmer as philosophical, ravaged Doc Holliday in <b>Tombstone</b>,
                  nor was Ben Kingsley as Schindler's Jewish accountant in <a href="schi.html"><b>Schindler's
                    List</b></a>, nor was Debra Winger as Arliss Howard's wife
                  Vida in a romantic triangle in the pyrokinetic, incendiary <b>Wilder
                  Napalm</b>.</font></p>
                <p><font size="2">Tim Burton's offbeat, stop-action animated musical <b>The
                  Nightmare Before Christmas</b> was nominated (and lost) for
                  only one award: Best Visual Effects, but was ignored for
                  Danny Elfman's original musical score, and for the songs
                  in its soundtrack (there were about a dozen songs in its
                  74 minutes). Unexpectedly, there were no nominations for
                  Wayne Wang's mother-daughter stories (adapted from Amy Tan's
                  novel) in <b>The Joy Luck Club</b>.</font></p>
                <p><font size="2">Juliette Binoche was denied a nomination as grieving
                  wife Julie Vignon de Courcy in the first film of director Krzysztof
                  Kieslowski's three-color trilogy, <b>Three Colors: Blue (Fr.)
                  (aka Trois Couleurs: Bleu)</b>.</font></p>
                <p><font size="2">Denzel Washington was denied an acting nomination
                  for his performance as black, ambulance-chasing defense lawyer
                  Joe Miller (opposite Best Actor-winning Tom Hanks) in <b>Philadelphia</b>,
                  as was Jeff Bridges for his role as post-traumatic stress disorder
                  victim Max Klein in Weir's <b>Fearless</b>, and Arnold Schwarzenegger
                  as fictional action hero film character Sgt. Jack Slater (and
                  himself) in <b>Last Action Hero</b>. Although nominated for <b>Remains
                    of the Day</b>, Anthony Hopkins should also have been nominated
                  for his role as Christian novelist C.S. Lewis in <b>Shadowlands</b> (a
                  film mostly neglected by the Academy). And Matthew McConaughey
                  was un-nominated for his memorable performance as the seasoned
                  suitor named Wooderson of Texas high school girls in <b>Dazed
                  and Confused</b>.</font></p>
                <p><font size="2">An emerging romantic comedy masterpiece, <b>Groundhog
                  Day</b>, struck out for Oscar nominations. There was no recognition
                  for director/screenwriter Harold Ramis, or for actor Bill
                  Murray's superb characterization as TV weatherman Phil who
                  was forced to repeat the same day over and over again in
                  an endless loop, and (supporting) actress Andie MacDowell
                  as TV producer Rita (Phil's love interest). Composer John
                  Williams' musical score for <b><a href="series-jurassicpark.html">Jurassic
                    Park</a></b> was also not nominated.</font></p>
          </td>
        </tr>
