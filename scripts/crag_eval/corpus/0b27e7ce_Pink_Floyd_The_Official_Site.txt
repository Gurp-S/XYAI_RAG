	<span class="h10">Remember a Day?</span>
      <div class="biotext">
			<br /><br />
            The early Sixties. Everything is up in the air, not least love, drugs 
            and sex. A group of talented teenagers from academic backgrounds in 
            Cambridge &#151; Roger 'Syd' Barrett, Roger Waters and David Gilmour &#151; 
            are all keen guitarists and among many who move to London, keen to 
            discover more of this new world and express themselves in it. Mainly 
            in further education &#151; studying the arts, architecture, music &#151; they 
            mix with like-minded incomers in the big city.<br />
            <br />
            In 1965, Barrett and Waters meet an experimental percussionist and 
            an extraordinarily gifted keyboards-player &#151; Nick Mason and Rick Wright 
            respectively. The result is Pink Floyd, which more than 40 years later 
            has moved from massive to almost mythic standing.<br />
            <br />
			Through several changes of personnel, through several musical phases, 
            the band has earned a place on the ultimate roll call of rock, along 
            with the Beatles, the Stones and Led Zeppelin. Their album sales have 
            topped 250 million. In 2005, at Live 8 &#151; the biggest global music 
            event in history &#151; the reunion of the four-man line-up that recorded 
            most of the Floyd canon stole the show. And yet, true to their beginnings, 
            there has always been an enigma at their heart.<br />
            <br />
			Roger 'Syd' Barrett, for example. This cool and charismatic son of 
            a university don was the original creative force behind the band (which 
            he named after the Delta bluesmen Pink Anderson and Floyd Council). 
            His vision was perfect for the times, and vice versa. He would lead 
            the band to its first precarious fame, and damage himself irreparably 
            along the way. And though the Floyd's Barrett era only lasted three 
            years, it always informed what they became.<br />
            <br />
			These were the summers of love, when LSD was less an hallucinogenic 
            interval than a lifestyle choice for some young people, who found 
            their culture in science fiction, the pastoral tradition, and a certain 
            strain of the Victorian imagination. Drawing on such themes, the elfin 
            Barrett wrote and sang on most of the early Floyd's material, which 
            made use of new techniques, such as tape-loops, feedback and echo 
            delay.<br />
            <br />
			Live, the Floyd played sonic freak-outs &#151; half-hidden by new-fangled 
            light-shows and projections &#151; with Barrett's spacey lead guitar swooping 
            over Waters' trance-like bass, while Wright and Mason created soundscapes 
            above and beneath. On record they were tighter, if still 'psychedelic'. 
            Either way, they sounded 'trippy'. And perhaps that was Barrett's 
            intention. He certainly ingested plenty of LSD and other drugs, which 
            didn't help his delicate mental balance.<br />
            <br />
			Over the spring of 1966, the young band were regulars at the Spontaneous 
            Underground 'happenings' on Sundays at the legendary Marquee Club, 
            where they were spotted by their future managers Peter Jenner and 
            Andrew King. And by the autumn, the Floyd had become the house band 
            of the so-called London Free School in west London.<br />
            <br />
            A semi-residency at the All Saint's Hall led to bigger bookings &#151; 
            at the UFO and the <i>International Times</i>' launch in the Roundhouse 
            &#151; as well as the recording of the instrumental 'Interstellar 
            Overdrive' with the UFO's co-founder, producer Joe Boyd. (This track 
            was later used on hip documentaries of the scene.) A signing to EMI 
            followed in early 1967.<br />
            <br />
			&quot;We want to be pop stars,&quot; said Syd. In March, Boyd recorded 
            Barrett's oddly commercial 'Arnold Layne' as a three-minute single. 
            And with a Top Twenty hit to promote, the band took on a gruelling 
            schedule of gigs and recordings.<br />
            <br />
			They appeared at the coolest event of the summer, The 14-Hour Technicolor 
            Dream in Alexandra Palace. They gave a concert under the banner 'Games 
            for May' in a classical venue &#151; the Queen Elizabeth Hall &#151; where they 
            displayed their theatrical ambitions through the use of props, pre-recorded 
            tapes and the world's first quadraphonic sound system. (They received 
            a lifetime ban for throwing daffodils into the audience.) And in June 
            the Floyd released a single originally written for this event.<br />
            <br />
            'See Emily Play', which was produced by EMI's Norman Smith, charted 
            at Number Six and made it on to primetime TV's Top of the Pops three 
            times (with Barrett acting increasingly strangely). This was followed 
            in August by Pink Floyd's first LP, <i>The Piper At The Gates of Dawn</i>, 
            which they recorded at Abbey Road next door to the Beatles, then working 
            on <i>Sergeant Pepper</i>. Again making the Top Ten, the album is 
            mainly Barrett's and is a precious relic of its time, a wonderful 
            mix of the whimsical and weird.<br />
            <br />
			Talking of which, Barrett's behaviour and output were threatening 
            to bring the band down with him: refusing to speak, playing one de-tuned 
            string all night, writing material like 'Scream Thy Last Scream, Old 
            Woman with a Basket'. The band wanted to keep their frontman and hoped 
            he would recover himself, so they asked David Gilmour &#151; now back in 
            London after a sojourn abroad &#151; to take over Syd's role on stage, 
            and thought Barrett might become their off-stage songwriter. They 
            tried a few gigs as a five-piece. But in the end, they decided they 
            could do without Barrett, and by March 1968 were in their second incarnation 
            and under new management.<br />
			<br />
			<br />
			<span class="h10">Set the Controls</span><br />
			<br />
            Barrett went his way with Jenner and King, and later recorded two 
            haunting solo albums &#151; on which Waters, Wright and especially 
            Gilmour helped &#151; before retreating to Cambridge for the rest 
            of his life. The other four acquired a new manager &#151; Steve O'Rourke 
            &#151; and in a state of some consternation finished their second 
            album, <i>A Saucerful of Secrets</i> (begun the previous year).<br />
            <br />
			Lyrical duties had now fallen to the bassist Roger Waters. And apart 
            from 'Jugband Blues' &#151; a disturbing track by Barrett, who contributed 
            little else &#151; the album's standout moments included the title track 
            and Waters' 'Set the Controls for the Heart of the Sun'.<br />
			<br />
            This hypnotic epic signposted the style the band would expand on in 
            the Seventies, its vision at first more appreciated by an 'intellectual' 
            and European audience. The Floyd played the first free concert in 
            Hyde Park, and laid down the soundtrack for the bizarre Paul Jones 
            movie vehicle, The Committee. They toured continually, developing 
            new material on stage as well as in the studio.<br />
			<br />
            And they worked on the experience, in April 1969 revealing an early 
            form of surround-sound at the Royal Festival Hall &#151; their rebuilt 
            'Azimuth Co-Ordinator'. (The prototype, first constructed and used 
            in 1967, had been stolen.) They worked on their concepts, too - at 
            that concert, performing two long pieces fusing old and new material, 
            entitled 'The Man' and 'The Journey'.<br />
            <br />
            So their star continued its inevitable ascent. In July, the Floyd 
            released More, less a soundtrack than an accompaniment to Barbet Schroder's 
            eponymous film about a group of hippies on the drug trail in Ibiza. 
            The same month, they played live 'atmospherics' to the BBC's live 
            coverage of the first moon landing. In November, they released the 
            double-album <i>Ummagumma</i>, a mixture of live and studio tracks 
            &#151; and that same month reworked its outstanding number, the eerie 
            'Careful With That Axe, Eugene', for Antonioni's cult film Zabriskie 
            Point.<br />
			<br />
            With <i>Ummagumma</i> at Number Five in the UK charts, and a growing 
            reputation in both Europe and the US underground, the Floyd played 
            some of the key festivals of their time &#151; Bath, Antibes, Rotterdam, 
            Montreux &#151; and between October 1970 and November 1971, put out 
            two more albums.<br />
            <br />
            <i>Atom Heart Mother</i>, their first Number One, featured the Floyd 
            in their pomp &#151; 'I like a bit of pomp,' says Gilmour (who also 
            made his first lyrical contribution with the gentle 'Fat Old Sun'). 
            And <i>Meddle</i> included two timeless and largely instrumental tracks 
            that showcased their lead guitarist in all his vertiginous, keening 
            glory: 'Echoes', which took up the whole of Side One and began with 
            a single 'ping' created almost accidentally by Wright, and 'One of 
            These Days'.<br />
			<br />
            Increasingly successful, in 1972 the band was still pushing the boundaries. 
            They shot the film 'Live at Pompei' in a Roman amphitheatre, recorded 
            another movie soundtrack for Schroder &#151; <i>Obscured by Clouds</i> 
            &#151; and performed with the Ballet de Marseille. But more importantly, 
            they began to work on an idea that would become their most popular 
            album and with 45 million sold, the world's third biggest.<br />
            <br />
            Provisionally entitled 'Eclipse' and honed through an extensive world 
            tour, <i>The Dark Side of the Moon</i> was released in March 1973, 
            and defies a potted critique here. Demonstrating Waters' talents as 
            both lyricist and conceptualist, it was also a musical <i>tour de 
            force</i> by Gilmour. But Waters was becoming <i>de facto</i> leader 
            of the band &#151; which in public at least was becoming less about 
            the individuals than the experience.<br />
            <br />
            That was (as Barrett had always intended) increasingly visual. The 
            intriguing sleeve artwork commissioned from the ex-Cambridge outfit 
            Hipgnosis was complemented by stage shows featuring crashing aeroplanes, 
            circular projection screens and flaming gongs. There were backing 
            singers on-stage and a guest slot for another pal from Cambridge, 
            the saxophonist Dick Parry. In the dawning age of stadium rock, the 
            Floyd were truly its masters.<br />
            <br />
            Or maybe its servants? Even before <i>Dark Side</i> broke Middle America 
            through FM radio &#151; with the single 'Money' &#151; alienation, 
            isolation and mental fragility had long been Waters' themes. As a 
            stadium performer, and a cog in the music business machine, he was 
            becoming more prone to all three. As Barrett's ex-colleague, he had 
            seen them embodied in his old friend. The results were evident in 
            two of his best lyrics &#151; for 'Shine On, You Crazy Diamond' and 
            'Wish You Were Here'. These tracks were the high points of the Floyd's 
            next LP, also called <i>Wish You Were Here</i>, which was begun in 
            January 1975 and released that summer.<br />
			<br />
            Famously, Barrett briefly appeared unannounced at Abbey Road during 
            the recording of 'Shine On' and shocked the band by his appearance 
            and demeanour. It was the last time any of them saw him &#151; but they 
            were seeing less of each other, too. Personal and musical differences 
            were starting to tell on the band, though it would be several years 
            until these became unbearable &#151; and two more LPs.<br />
			<br />
			<br />
			<span class="h10">Which One's Pink?</span><br />
			<br />
            The first was <i>Animals</i>, released in January 1977 (although work 
            had also begun on it in 1975). When this was toured with lavish special 
            effects, including giant inflatables, Waters was dismayed that the 
            crowds kept calling for old hits. In Montreal his patience snapped 
            and he spat into the audience. It was a cathartic moment that gave 
            birth to the Floyd's most ambitious project ever: <i>The Wall</i>, 
            a largely autobiographical reflection by Waters on the nature of love, 
            life and art.<br />
            <br />
			The double album charts the progress of a rock star, 'Pink', facing 
            the break-up of his marriage while on tour. This leads him to review 
            his life from the death of his father - like Waters' killed on the 
            battlefield before he was born - to his spiteful teachers, his business, 
            even his audience. He sees each as a brick in a metaphorical wall 
            between him and the rest of the world. This wall intensifies his isolation, 
            until he imagines the only solution is to become a fascist dictator. 
            When he confronts his madness and deals with his issues, his torments 
            cease and the wall crumbles.<br />
            <br />
			The show &#151; in which the band were slowly obscured by a giant wall 
            of cardboard 'bricks' &#151; was the most ambitious the rock world had 
            ever seen, and was also turned into an Alan Parker film, starring 
            Bob Geldof (who would return to the Floyd story 25 years later). The 
            album sold 20 million, and spawned the band's only Number One single, 
            the anti-authoritarian 'Another Brick in the Wall, Part 2'.<br />
			<br />
            Though the album had its musical highlights &#151; Gilmour's solo on 'Comfortably 
            Numb' being the most memorable &#151; it was largely a lyrical piece. Waters 
            drove the project and the others fitted in. They ceded their vision 
            to his increasingly personal direction, and worked together on no 
            new material for more than two years.<br />
			<br />
            When they did get back in the studio, it was to record <i>The Final 
            Cut</i>. This prophetically titled album, prompted by the Falklands 
            conflict of 1982 and released the next year, explores themes of remembrance 
            and the undelivered post-war dream &#151; for which Waters' father 
            had given his life. Completely credited to Waters, it was attributed 
            to 'Roger Waters, performed by Pink Floyd' and featured Gilmour's 
            vocals on one track.<br />
			<br />
            After three years &#151; during which all four band members had pursued 
            solo projects &#151; Waters announced he was leaving the Floyd and 
            disbanding them. Wright had left the legal entity some time before, 
            transferring to the payroll for <i>The Wall</i> tour and playing no 
            part in <i>The Final Cut</i>, but Gilmour and Mason decided to continue 
            Pink Floyd without its erstwhile 'leader'. A turbulent period followed, 
            but agreement was eventually reached: Waters would continue to perform 
            the songs on which he worked while he was with the band, as well as 
            new solo material. Gilmour &#151; now first among equals &#151; and 
            Mason would continue to record and perform with Wright as Pink Floyd.<br />
			<br />
            In 1987 came their next album, <i>A Momentary Lapse of Reason</i> 
            &#151; which emphatically proved that the Floyd could exist without 
            Waters. The subsequent world tour, which also spawned the live <i>Delicate 
            Sound of Thunder</i>, was the band's longest and most successful ever. 
            Over four years, 5.5 million people saw 200 shows, including one on 
            a floating stage in Venice (which again earned them a venue-ban) while 
            <i>Thunder</i> became the first rock album to be played in space, 
            by the Soviet-French Soyuz-7 mission.<br />
			<br />
            1994's album and tour, <i>The Division Bell</i>, broke similar records; 
            but more, it showed Gilmour and the band on a creative roll, with 
            Wright contributing to some of the writing and Gilmour forging a new 
            writing partnership with his wife, the novelist Polly Samson &#151; 
            'High Hopes' being one of their new classics. However, since then, 
            the Floyd has recorded no new material in the studio.<br />
			<br />
            Not that they have been inactive &#151; nor untouched by sorrows. 
            In 2003, the band's manager Steve O'Rourke died from a stroke and 
            the three-man Floyd played 'Fat Old Sun' and <i>Dark Side</i>'s 'Great 
            Gig in the Sky', at his funeral in Chichester Cathedral. In 2006, 
            Syd Barrett died from pancreatic cancer. And in 2008 Rick Wright followed 
            him &#151; but not before he had helped re-write the Pink Floyd story 
            a couple more times.<br />
            <br />
            In 2005, prompted by Bob Geldof, the band decided to perform at Live 
            8 (on the 20th anniversary of Live Aid) and invited Waters to join 
            them. He accepted and &#151; sharing vocals with Gilmour &#151; they 
            played two numbers from <i>Dark Side</i>, plus 'Wish You Were Here' 
            and 'Comfortably Numb'. It was an epoch-making moment in rock history, 
            and their final group hug became one of Live 8's iconic images.<br />
			<br />
            After that, the three-man Floyd performed together on two occasions 
            &#151; once during a solo gig by Gilmour in 2006 (Wright played the whole 
            three-month tour and was 'in great form', says Gilmour); and again 
            at an all-star memorial tribute to Barrett in 2007. Waters also appeared 
            at the gig but was unable to join his old colleagues due to a previous 
            appointment. Still, that was not the end of their association.<br />
			<br />
            On 10 July 2010, with some of their favourite musicians, Waters and 
            Gilmour performed a few Floyd songs &#151; plus Phil Spector's 'To 
            Know Him Is To Love Him'! &#151; at a private charity event in Oxfordshire. 
            And on 12 May 2011, during one of Waters' <i>Wall</i> concerts at 
            the London O2, Gilmour appeared on top of the wall as of old, to sing 
            and play his parts on 'Comfortably Numb'. Nick Mason, who was at the 
            gig, then joined them for the final song, 'Outside the Wall'. Departing 
            the stage, as they had before, Waters played trumpet, Gilmour mandolin 
            and Mason tambourine. The audience was stunned and delighted.<br />
			<br />
            But a handful of concerts was never going to sate the interest of 
            the diehard fans. In 1995, they were rewarded with the double-album 
            <i>P&#149;U&#149;L&#149;S&#149;E</i>, all recorded on the <i>Division Bell</i> tour and 
            containing the first complete live version of <i>Dark Side</i>. A 
            live compilation of <i>The Wall</i> from 1980-1 &#151; called <i>Is 
            There Anybody Out There?</i> &#151; followed in 2000, and then a re-mastered 
            'best of', called <i>Echoes</i>. There have also been collectors' 
            editions of <i>Dark Side</i>, a complete works box-set &#151; <i>Oh, 
            By the Way</i> &#151; and now (autumn 2011) an extensive reissue campaign 
            by EMI, with new packaging and production values, not to mention some 
            rare and archival recordings that go back to the Barrett days.<br />
			<br />
            Nor, as individuals, have the survivors from those times been strangers 
            to the studio or stage these last dozen or so years (and before). 
            Gilmour put out his third solo album, <i>On an Island</i>, in 2006; 
            Waters has had a prolific and varied career since 1986; Mason and 
            Wright released one or two collaborative albums respectively.<br />
			<br />
            There have been awards and honours along the way: induction into both 
            the US and UK Rock 'n' Roll Halls of Fame; Sweden's Polar Music Prize 
            in 2008 for their 'monumental contribution over the decades to the 
            fusion of art and music in the development of popular culture'. And 
            in 2010, The Royal Mail used <i>Division Bell</i> visuals on their 
            stamps, also creating a unique sheet using only the Floyd's imagery.<br />
			<br />
            So is that the end of the Floyd's road? Do they still exist? Yes, they do.<p>&nbsp;</p>
